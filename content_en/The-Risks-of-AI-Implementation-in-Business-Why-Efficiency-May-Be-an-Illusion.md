One of today’s biggest misconceptions seems to be that if your team works faster and produces more content or writes more code, its productivity must have increased.

From the outside, it really can look that way. AI generates documents, text, program code, and brainstorming ideas hundreds of times faster than people do. Sounds great!

> *— And the quality doesn’t drop? a business owner might wonder.*

That is the question... A person responsible for the outcome still has to check whatever AI generates. If a website goes down after an update, for example, users will lose trust and the business will suffer reputational damage. The AI is unlikely to be the one held accountable.

So yes, we can generate enormous amounts of content in a short time. But does that really make an employee—or the department as a whole—more efficient?

At a recent birthday party, a friend who works somewhere between QA and product told me about a project her team is building. Their service regularly adds to and rewrites its own code. It releases updates twice a day.

Now put yourself in her place. Twice a day, she gets a modified product with new features, and no one knows whether those features work correctly or were just an AI hallucination in the report. Delight and pain!

My friend described it more accurately: AI hysteria. Checking dozens of new features and fixes at that speed is incredibly stressful.

## The Business World Has Accelerated Again. What Did We Sacrifice?

When a field makes a sudden leap, a correction period should follow as market participants adapt to the new trend. A strong trend impulse is always offset by something.

In our case, the price is a steep drop in product quality. Nothing generated by AI today is suitable for blind deployment. The people who used to write articles, code, and specifications or create designs and graphics are still here. But their numbers have fallen by tens, if not hundreds of thousands of times. Most of the people who remain are busy checking what AI generates. That is the real business bottleneck.

Today, the average worker in almost any digital profession uses AI for tasks they once did themselves. Now they reread pages of change reports, ask for corrections anywhere from two to twenty times—and that’s the best case—and configure agents. They lose focus when AI changes far more than requested. Every day, they push a dozen mediocre outputs through their brains. In other words, they do work that lowers efficiency in practice.

In a [METR study](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/), for example, experienced open-source developers expected AI to make them about 24% faster. In reality, the tasks took 19% longer. After the experiment, participants still believed AI had sped them up.

I find this particularly interesting. When we type less by hand and get something half-finished more quickly, it’s very easy to conclude that the work as a whole is moving faster. It reminds me of a school student learning to program who finds it easier to select code with a mouse, click the context menu, copy, and paste than to type `print` by hand—even though the first sequence takes three or four times as long.

The speed has indeed increased, but it is the speed at which people’s brains have to review generated content. Of course, that has side effects. And this is where it gets interesting.

## AI Brain Fry: When AI Increases ~~Efficiency~~ the Risk of Overload

[Harvard Business Review](https://hbr.org/2026/03/when-using-ai-leads-to-brain-fry?ab=HP-hero-for-you-2) has already described this as AI brain fry. The BCG authors draw on a study of [1,488 employees at large US companies](https://www.bcg.com/news/5march2026-when-using-ai-leads-brain-fry) and discuss attention overload, more errors, decision overload, and the desire to quit.

The same article quotes a Gas Town user:

> *Too much going on for you to reasonably comprehend.*

Put more plainly:

> *There is too much happening to make sense of it.*

I think that captures the situation very precisely. We’re used to thinking that automation reduces the workload. In practice, agents and generative tools often add another layer of oversight:

- They have to be constrained to prevent junk output.
- The result has to be interpreted because its meaning is not always clear.
- Errors often have to be investigated in the live product rather than a test environment.

[LeadDev](https://leaddev.com/ai/addictive-agentic-coding-has-developers-losing-sleep) describes this well through the example of coding agents. And everyone in the article enjoys it. They start spending longer at the computer, keep working at the same pace on weekends, and live with the feeling that they could get just a little more done. It is a vicious circle. Really, it is FOMO.

For a business owner, the risk is that cognitive overload rarely remains one employee’s personal problem. After a while, it starts affecting the quality of middle managers’ decisions and the team’s shared understanding of what is happening.

Working with AI creates **workslop**—from *work* and *slop*. Researchers at Stanford and BetterUp Labs introduced the term to describe junk content.

BetterUp Labs and the Stanford Social Media Lab use it for AI-generated content or tasks that look professional and polished but offer no real value. In their sample of [150 full-time desk workers](https://www.betterup.com/workslop), about 40% had encountered workslop in the previous month, and a single instance took an average of around two hours to deal with.

That is the problem. AI does remove some cheap labor. But it does not do a person’s entire job. First of all, it changes the production process within a company. Only then can it make things faster—and only if it is introduced thoughtfully. So what are the risks?

Take an average company whose established processes depend heavily on the human factor. In that environment, AI can quickly:

- increase the volume of questionable changes;
- add hidden work for CTOs, tech leads, product managers, and QA;
- reduce the quality of code or content while increasing employees’ working hours.

In the [official announcement of the 2025 DORA report](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/announcing-the-2025-dora-report), Google Cloud writes:

> AI adoption continues to have a negative relationship with software delivery stability.

And its [DevOps page](https://cloud.google.com/devops) puts it succinctly:

> AI is an amplifier, not a fix.

I like that idea. In a well-designed process that depends little on human factors, AI really can provide a benefit. In a weak process, it can accelerate not just releases but also errors and organizational noise, while increasing the cost of everyone else’s attention.

Consider public incidents at large corporations. They show the reality that modern technology companies operate in: even very strong teams continually work against a background of degradation, outages, regressions, and vulnerabilities.

The [GitHub Status history](https://www.githubstatus.com/history) for April 2026 alone includes a Pull Requests regression that could cause the merge queue to release updates incorrectly, a separate Pages incident where the status page reports [about 17.5 million failed requests](https://www.githubstatus.com/api/v2/incidents.json), and a Codespaces outage with [an error rate of about 40% when project builds started](https://www.githubstatus.com/api/v2/incidents.json).

The [OpenAI Status history](https://status.openai.com/history) for April 2026 includes:

- Codex unresponsive
- Elevated conversation errors
- Login issues
- File upload failures
- and separate issues affecting various products.

Vercel has an [official status history](https://www.vercel-status.com/) and an [April 2026 security bulletin](https://vercel.com/kb/bulletin/vercel-april-2026-security-incident) that explicitly mentions authorization errors in internal systems. Its status page also records [critical errors in the dashboard and API endpoints](https://www.vercel-status.com/api/v2/incidents.json) during the same period, which caused some users to have problems building new versions of their applications.

I think modern systems are already fragile and expensive to maintain. Increasing the stream of changes without restraint is a questionable idea even without AI. I would approach AI adoption by looking specifically at how it changes a team’s working pace:

- Limit the number of parallel AI workstreams, especially if the team already lacks time for proper reviews.
- Work out how much time the team can spend checking AI output without other tasks falling apart.
- Track defects, incidents, workload, and the quality of decisions.
- Require tests and security checks for generated changes.
- Always have a person responsible for the final result.
- Regularly ask the team where AI genuinely makes work faster and where it merely creates noise.
- Don’t use AI to automate one isolated part of a process. Redesign the process around its use.

## Conclusion

As a developer, I enjoy seeing the world through a technical lens. I can’t say I feel negatively about AI. But I don’t really understand the excitement about processes moving faster, either. That speed brings a lot of side effects. In this context, increased efficiency is usually an illusion. I configure agent systems for my own work and for clients. And I can tell you that very few companies are ready to redesign processes they have spent years building and retrain their employees. It genuinely hurts.

For a business owner, the main question is whether the company can manage teams working at higher speed without losing control. It seems to me that self-regulation and deliberate attention management could become some of the most valuable skills of the next few years. They would allow anyone who regularly works with digital technology to maintain and improve their mental, emotional, and physical health. Only then does it make sense to talk about becoming more effective in business and at work.
