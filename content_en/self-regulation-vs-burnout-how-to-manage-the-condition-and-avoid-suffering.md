## Why This Matters More Than Any Time-Management System

I know people who maintain the perfect task manager. They constantly monitor their email, run weekly retrospectives, and set clear priorities. And they still hate every Monday morning.

The problem isn’t their tools. It isn’t discipline, either. The problem is their state. That is where any discussion of a skill should begin—before productivity, goals, communication, and everything else in this series.

Everything I write about here came to me through friendships and reading nontechnical literature. And—to my own surprise—it turned out to be especially relevant to technical people. There is probably nothing completely new here. Many people know these ideas. But of every ten who know, only one or two act on them.

When a person feels tired, anxious, unmotivated, irritable, or empty inside, the first reaction is almost always the same. They look for an external solution.

They think: I need to plan better. I need to stop being lazy. Pull myself together. Rest for a couple of days and get back to it.

Sometimes that helps briefly. More often, it doesn’t, because the problem lies deeper.

When people burn out, it isn’t so much their supply of strength that becomes depleted as their system of internal regulation. They may keep getting things done. Keep going to work. Appear composed and strong.

But their inner foundation is already breaking down. Their thinking becomes less clear. They feel and understand their needs less. Anxiety accumulates, attention narrows, and more of life happens “on autopilot,” without conscious thought. Their internal responses become harder and harder to notice.

Recovery begins by reestablishing contact with your inner state.

## Where Results Come From

During my first year at university, a chain of chance encounters led me to someone who made a strong impression on me at the time.

He was in his early forties: an accomplished entrepreneur, an investor, and a happy family man. What surprised me most was that he didn’t seem like a horse driven into the ground. He didn’t look as though he was holding on by his last thread. It felt as though all the parts of his life somehow fit together.

To be honest, I envied him—in a good way. My persistence led to several valuable meetings with him.

He said something I wrote down at the time but didn’t truly understand until several years later:

> “Most people think results depend on knowledge and skills. But knowledge and skills account for ten percent at most. The other ninety percent is beliefs and your internal state. Those are the things people pay the least attention to.”

At the time, I didn’t really understand what that meant in practice. But I remembered his words and later began to notice the pattern in real life.

One example of how our internal state affects long-term decisions is [my 12-year journey through education and into a different line of work](/blog/leaving-the-field-of-it-education).

He broke the numbers down like this:

- 5% — skills
- 5% — knowledge
- 40% — beliefs
- 50% — internal state

I don’t know how rigorous those figures are. They seem quite subjective, but years of practice have convinced me that the proportions are roughly right. That is why working with your state isn’t some optional soft skill. It is the foundation that holds everything else up—or fails to.

You can know a great deal but lack the strength to apply it. You can have the skills but lose your bearings under pressure. You can be talented yet live with constant inner tension, spending all your energy on an internal struggle instead of action. You can be capable of many things yet become so disconnected from yourself that neither your knowledge nor your skills work.

## What No One Can See from the Outside

Picture this. Somewhere in a cold northern sea, a piece of ice rises from the water—tall, white, and faintly blue in the light. It looks imposing. Waves spread slowly around it. That is all an observer on a ship can see.

But beneath the water is a different story. Eight to ten times more ice lies below the surface: a vast, silent mass hidden from view. That mass determines where the entire iceberg moves. Not the wind or the waves, but the part underwater.

Above the water is everything others can see: our actions, decisions, words, external results, and stated goals. The things people judge us by. The things we present to the world.

Below the water lies most of the mass. Our habitual way of thinking. The focus of our attention. Suppressed experiences. Automatic responses to stress. Internal conflicts we don’t see but which constantly pull at our resources. Hidden fears. Subconscious goals—the ones we don’t admit even to ourselves. And deep beliefs about ourselves and the world that we never chose. They simply formed at some point and have been running in the background ever since, quietly controlling our reactions.

Very few people track any of this. Most work only with the visible part.

We try to change our behavior without noticing our state. We try to change the result without noticing our thinking. We try to become more effective without noticing that energy has been quietly leaking inside us for a long time. We work on the part of the iceberg above the water and wonder why it doesn’t change direction.

## Processes

Your internal processes shape the reality in which you live. That sounds strange, I agree. Let me unpack it.

The way you think—how you explain events to yourself, what you believe, where you direct your attention, what you consider possible, what you fear, how you interpret your mistakes, and how you treat yourself in moments of weakness—programs your reactions, decisions, and actions. Those reactions, decisions, and actions create your external circumstances. That is the mechanism.

A person convinced that they always do everything wrong will make different decisions from someone who knows that mistakes are part of the process. A person living with constant anxiety will respond differently to the same event than someone with a stable inner baseline. The external world you create around yourself is, to a large degree, an extension of your inner structure.

Let’s use our imagination again and picture an old acoustic instrument—a cello, perhaps. A warm wooden body, taut strings, a bow. You pick it up. It is beautiful: complex, delicate, and powerful. But when you draw the bow across the strings, the sound is false. Not because you can’t play, but because the instrument is out of tune.

If the strings are too tight, they will sound harsh and eventually snap. If they are too loose, you’ll get a dull slap instead of a note. Leave the instrument untuned for too long and it will sound false even in a virtuoso’s hands. No amount of skill can compensate.

Something very similar happens with our minds. The way we are tuned is the way we sound in life. Our internal organization shapes our actions. The way we talk to ourselves shapes how we experience difficulty. The way we manage attention determines how we distribute our energy.

But you can’t tune an instrument without understanding how it works.

<a id="what-energy-really-is"></a>

## What Energy Really Is

When I hear someone say, “I have no energy,” I now hear something else. Let me explain. Here is the formula: **stored energy == the amount of attention directed inward**.

You can get enough sleep and still feel empty by the middle of the day. You can go away for a week and return with the same inner tension you took with you. You can clear an entire day, lie down on the sofa, do nothing, and feel even more depleted two hours later. Why?

Try to remember what it feels like to hold something heavy at arm’s length for a long time. At first, nothing happens. Then your arm begins to burn. Then it starts shaking. Eventually you can’t lift it. The object hasn’t become heavier, but your arm can no longer hold it. That is how attention works when it is constantly directed outward.

Toward other people’s expectations—what they will think, how they will judge you, whether you will disappoint them. Toward controlling external circumstances—what happens if I don’t finish in time, what happens if something goes wrong. Toward a constant stream of tasks and notifications that demand a response.

Toward information noise—the news, social networks, and messaging apps we open automatically. Toward the need to measure up to team expectations, standards, and the image of ourselves we project. Toward anxious forecasting—an endless internal monologue about what might go wrong. Toward comparison with others—who has already succeeded, who is moving faster, who looks more confident.

When attention remains outside all the time, a person gradually loses contact with themselves. Once that contact is gone, they stop noticing that they have been overloaded for a long time. They keep working “on credit.” They keep saying yes to new tasks. They keep up the pace. One day, they realize they are still working but no longer feel anything.

Energy returns when we bring our attention back to ourselves. That is the most practical thing I know about this subject.

<a id="why-external-life-is-so-draining"></a>

## Why External Life Is So Draining

Our brains are constantly busy with one important task: trying to bring order to external chaos. The brain needs clarity and predictability. It wants to understand what is happening, what will happen next, what it can rely on, and where danger lies. This consumes an enormous amount of energy, continuously and in the background.

We want everything to be linear. If I try hard, I’ll get a result. If I do everything right, I’ll be appreciated. If I think everything through, nothing bad will happen. If I organize everything, the tension will disappear.

But life is nonlinear. And this is where one of our deepest internal conflicts emerges.

We look for stability in the absence of change. But real life consists of change. Nothing is more stable than change.

That may sound paradoxical. Yet this is what mature inner support looks like. It isn’t the expectation that everything will finally become perfectly predictable. It is the ability to remain steady inside a world that constantly changes.

Sometimes chaos feels like a threat. But we can look at it differently: chaos is a higher form of order that we are not yet able to understand fully. When a person begins to feel this—not just understand it intellectually, but actually feel it—the frantic need to control everything around them recedes. A different skill takes its place: the ability to rely on yourself in a changing world.

<a id="three-foundational-skills"></a>

## Three Foundational Skills

Before discussing practices, we need to understand what supports them. The three skills at the center of this discussion all sound familiar. In reality, people tend to understand them differently.

### Stress Resilience

People usually take this to mean the ability to endure pressure.

In the common view, a “stress-resilient person” is someone who doesn’t break under pressure. They keep up appearances, don’t complain, and continue working.

That isn’t stress resilience. It is chronic suppression.

**Stress resilience** is the ability to adapt to new conditions and demands. It is also the calm and awareness accumulated within you.

**From the outside, it might look like this:**

You notice what is happening to you. You don’t deny the tension. You don’t fall into an automatic reaction: you don’t explode, withdraw, or start acting from a state of panic. You retain the ability to think, feel, and choose.

The difference between “putting up with it” and “being resilient” is fundamental. A person who merely endures keeps accumulating pressure. Sooner or later, it comes out as a breakdown, illness, or burnout. A resilient person doesn’t accumulate it, because they experience what is happening instead of suppressing it.

### Self-Organization

This term is almost always associated with discipline, schedules, and task lists. That isn’t wrong, but it is only the surface.

Self-organization is the ability to manage your own time, motivate yourself, and manage your resources to reach goals without external control. More precisely, it is the organization of your physical and psychological environment for your own benefit.

The emphasis is on **“for your own benefit.”**

You can keep a tidy calendar and meet every deadline while falling apart inside. You can live in a state of chronic overstrain. You can do everything right and abuse yourself every day.

Real **self-organization** means creating an internal and external environment that makes it easier to remain clear, recover, concentrate, and act—without constantly forcing yourself through pain.

This is why some of the most useful questions to ask yourself regularly are: what is good for me? What is genuinely in my best interest right now? What helps me live sustainably? Reflect on the answers. The power of open questions grows over the years.

### Self-Reflection

**Self-reflection** is the process of examining your thoughts, emotions, actions, and motives so that you can look at yourself from the outside.

In other words, it is observing yourself and seeing causes and consequences clearly.

It is the ability to notice what you are thinking and experiencing right now, why you respond in a particular way, what comes alive inside you in a given situation, and what consequences your internal reactions have.

Without self-reflection, a person lives on autopilot. They react automatically. They fall into the same situations again and again without understanding why. They feel that something is wrong but can’t find a place to begin.

With self-reflection, room for choice appears. A gap opens between stimulus and response where you can pause and decide: how do I want to respond? What is true for me right now?

For some reason, many people confuse this with unhealthy introspection. Don’t fall into that trap. Don’t judge your actions as good or bad, and don’t compare your results with other people’s. Instead, simply observe yourself and your internal processes without judgment—build your familiarity with them, so to speak.

### The Illusion That Drains Us

There is a phrase I return to regularly. It comes from the Stoics but sounds surprisingly modern: people are disturbed not by things themselves, but by their thoughts about those things.

Imagine standing at a window and looking out through dirty glass. There is rain, people, and cars outside—everything as usual. But the streaks and smudges on the window distort it all. You see what is there plus every distortion in the glass. The dirtier the glass, the greater the distortion.

The internal layer of interpretation is the glass between us and events. Something happens. Then the questions begin: how did I explain this to myself? What did I think? What meaning did I give it? Which story from my own life did I connect it to? We don’t see the event itself. We see it through our own glass.

This layer often creates most of the overload, and our beliefs govern it.

The same fact—a hard deadline, a production bug, an unflattering code review comment, or silence in the chat after something you said—can be perceived in completely different ways. It can look like a catastrophe and proof that you aren’t good enough. Or a challenge you can handle. Or a temporary difficulty that will pass. Or an experience you can learn from. Everyone explains the events in their life differently.

It is important not to swing to the other extreme. Circumstances, workloads, and conflicts are real. But the way we experience those circumstances is shaped in large part by what happens inside us. And that is something we can genuinely influence.

### Inside and Outside

Here is a simple image. Take a flashlight and point the beam away from yourself, into the room. You see everything around you: walls, furniture, objects. Turn the flashlight toward yourself—just not into your eyes—and you finally see where you are, what you’re holding, what you’re standing on, and what state you’re in.

Attention works the same way. Outside are your actions. Inside is the focus of your attention.

If the flashlight of attention is always pointed outward, a person lives only in reaction mode. It is as if they are constantly processing external signals: emails, tasks, demands, expectations, fears, deadlines, notifications, and other people’s emotions. A constant incoming stream and a constant response.

In this mode, a person can be extremely busy. Active. Productive over a short period. And completely disconnected from themselves at the same time.

When even part of the beam turns inward, something important happens. You can feel tired before you break down, not after. You can notice that anxiety has already taken hold of your body. You can recognize that you are acting from tension rather than clarity—and that is an important difference. You can separate your true goal from an automatic attempt to earn approval. You can avoid losing yourself in all the external motion.

Self-regulation is the ability to remain in close enough contact with yourself that you can gently and accurately return to stable ground. To notice when the current is carrying you away and come back—not by force, but through attention.

## Five Steps of Inner Work

As soon as you start talking about managing your state, people want a technique. What exactly should I do? Give me an exercise. That makes sense: we’re used to concrete solutions.

But a technique without an understanding of the process works for two days. Then the person decides that “it isn’t for them” and returns to where they started. So the logic comes first. The practice follows.

Working with your inner state always moves through five consecutive steps. Skip any one of them and jump straight to the last, and you are guaranteed not to get a result.

### Step One: Sensing

Everything begins when I notice that something is happening. No analysis, judgment, or explanation yet. I simply register the signal. Tension in my shoulders, as if someone had placed their hands on them and were pressing down. A heaviness in my chest—dull rather than sharp, but constant. A scattered feeling that won’t pass—my eyes slide across the screen but nothing holds them. Irritation without an obvious cause—when a colleague writes something neutral and something tightens inside me.

This is the first and perhaps most important step. Anything we don’t notice cannot be regulated.

It sounds banal, but most people are deeply numb to their own state. They notice only the extremes: when things are already terrible or after they have exploded. Everything in between passes them by. The ability to notice subtle signals is a skill. It can be trained. And it is where everything else begins.

### Step Two: Understanding

Once you notice something happening, the next step is to name it. What exactly is happening to me? Am I tired? Anxious? Angry? Overloaded? Do I feel powerless? Am I trying to hold too many things at once?

Once a state has a name, it stops being a shapeless threat. A brain with a word for an experience processes it differently from a brain that merely senses “something bad.” Neuroscience supports this: affect labeling reduces the intensity of an emotional response. But you don’t need the neuroscience. Try it. Give the state a name and see what changes.

### Step Three: Finding Patterns

Patterns begin to emerge at this stage. In which situations does this happen? What happened just before? Which people were around me? What pace was I living at? Which thoughts was I having? How was I working?

Now a map of the terrain appears. “I feel bad sometimes” becomes “I feel worse after three meetings in a row without a break,” or “this state appears whenever I’m about to do something where people may judge me.” A map gives you power. Once you see a pattern, you can no longer pretend that “it just happens.”

### Step Four: Awareness

Now you can see causes and consequences. What triggers my stress? What intensifies it? Which of my beliefs feed this state?

For example: “I’m afraid of being judged because at some point I tied recognition to my value as a person. Now, whenever I have a demo or a review coming up, that belief activates and I become anxious even when everything is objectively fine.”

At this stage, you uncover things that were difficult to see before. Sometimes they’re uncomfortable. Sometimes they’re unexpected. But without this step, you can only manage symptoms, not causes.

### Step Five: Management

Only after the first four steps does real management become possible. Not suppression. Not restraint. A free choice about how to respond to the situation.

How can I move through this state? What can I change—in the situation, in my beliefs, or in my pace? What would be helpful to me now? How can I use this understanding for my own benefit?

This is the opposite of an automatic response. In an automatic response, the body and our habits answer for us before we have time to think. Management means there is a gap between stimulus and response. We are present in that gap.

Most people try to skip the first four steps and go straight to the fifth. They want to “just manage it.” But management is impossible without sensing, understanding, finding patterns, and awareness. It is like trying to fix a program without reading the logs.

<a id="conclusion"></a>

## Conclusion

If I had to condense all of this into one thought, it would look something like this:

- First, feel the state
- Then name it
- Notice the situations that repeat
- Understand the cause

Only then should you change your response. When this order is broken, a person once again tries to fix the effect while the problem lies deeper.

This is especially important for work, business, and learning. Knowledge and skills produce results only when a person has the inner support, attention, and energy to apply them. Without that, even a good tool becomes one more way to put greater pressure on yourself.

The first step is small:

1. Bring some of your attention back inside.
2. Catch the signal before it becomes a breakdown.
3. Name the state honestly.
4. Notice where the same pattern repeats.

This is where management begins. Instead of rigid control, we learn to stay in contact with ourselves. Over time, that restores clarity, resilience, and the ability to choose our response in the moment.
