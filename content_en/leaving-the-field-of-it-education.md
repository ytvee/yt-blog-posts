## How It All Began

When I was a first-year college student in Almaty, the husband of a woman I knew gave her a children’s education center as a birthday gift. Her name is Natalia. This happened at the beginning of my second year. I was studying in the VTiPO faculty. At the end of that academic year, Natalia’s robotics teacher quit without notice. That evening, she waited for me outside my building until I came home from class. She explained what had happened and asked me to teach robotics to children.

> YOU’RE A PROGRAMMER!

I had serious doubts. I didn’t just lack experience; I had no idea how to work with children at all. She reassured me that they would teach me everything. Useful experience and at least some money, I thought, and agreed.

That is more or less what happened. There were plenty of experienced teachers at the center. After my own classes, I would go to them with a notebook and bombard them with questions. I didn’t feel like reading books on pedagogy at the time because I saw the job strictly as a side gig.

I worked through the whole season quite methodically. Before I left, the parents of one boy thanked me warmly for my work and gave me a Turkish shirt. It was a WOW moment. I think that was when the idea first took hold that this might be my calling. But it was an emotional thought, more something to consider than a definite decision.

Then the new academic year began, followed by a curious chain of events.

Throughout my third year, I read books on pedagogy and psychology alongside my college work. In my fourth year, I ran my first practical psychology workshop at the suggestion of my microprocessors teacher. That same year, she defended her thesis for a second degree. I think my new interest happened to coincide nicely with the period when she was preparing for her defense. She got involved and helped me design the workshop program.

That year, company owners also visited our college to select potential hires. Eight out of ten teachers recommended me. I spent the remaining time before university working at a company that developed document management software. It was called Lab of the Future. It was the most boring job of my life.

Then I moved to Saint Petersburg, still thinking that programming in an office was dull and tedious. That was when I finally decided to work in education. The decision was still emotional. Even so, I liked the work.

I began reading more and taking both online and in-person courses in pedagogy. I worked part-time at children’s centers while studying applied computer science. Of course, my view of my path has changed a great deal since then...

I had students I’m still in touch with today. Some are only considering becoming developers, while others already do paid work. I even once “let go” of a student who had been working on my projects so he could join a better-paid one. It hurt to see him leave, but I was proud.

Over those years, I managed to...

work in schools and education centers, teach one-on-one and in families and camps, both online and in person.

I taught children as young as four, all the way up to university students.

The smart and the lazy, those hungry to learn and those who had to be persuaded. Children with additional needs too—and there were quite a few of them.

All that time, it wasn’t so much a job as... Well, it depended on the period. Sometimes it paid the bills; sometimes it was simply a side job. But at its heart, it was service.

## The Change Within Me

<div align="center">* * *</div>

![education-feedback-1](https://res.cloudinary.com/dtdrbhksw/image/upload/v1784981452/opt-we_qjm9vj.png)

<div align="center">* * *</div>

I still remember the teachers from my childhood. I can count them on one hand. But I remember those people’s presence in my education with immense gratitude. They set an example for me.

<div align="center">* * *</div>

<PostVideo id="jasa-kids" />

<div align="center">* * *</div>

When I started teaching myself, I decided that I wanted to be like them. Not “just another teacher who only knows theory,” not an arrogant mentor, but someone the children would remember when they grew up. Someone they would remember fondly.

**I believe I succeeded.**

And you know what? It was fascinating.

<div align="center">* * *</div>

<PostVideo id="online-lesson" />

<div align="center">* * *</div>

We went through the full range of emotions in class: joy, excitement, anger, laughter, and sometimes even tears. There was even more of that at the camps. All of it became a treasure trove of moments that stay with you and make life feel fuller.

The variety was a huge bonus: Kazakhstan, Russia, England, Switzerland, and India.

Every country has its own mentality and its own particular traits. I taught lessons back to back: one for ₽200, the next for ₽5,000. To me, it...

made no difference. Not in how I treated the students, nor in how they treated me.

Then, in 2023, I felt that I finally had the strength to let go.

<div align="center">* * *</div>

<PostVideo id="memories" />

<div align="center">* * *</div>

For a couple of years, I tried to switch completely to a different line of work. It only began to feel natural in 2025.

I wrote separately about how I now understand the connection between our inner state, fatigue, and burnout in [“Self-Regulation vs. Burnout: How to Manage Your State Without Suffering”](/blog/self-regulation-vs-burnout-how-to-manage-the-condition-and-avoid-suffering).

Of course, I still have much to learn about humility and patience.
But contemplation and self-observation are more valuable than any material possession. I described the details and methods in my first Habr article, [“5 Outcomes of Learning in IT and Beyond”](https://habr.com/p/854096/).

Those 12 years were a long journey. They made me a different person, and I’m grateful for that. I still have a pile of feedback and memorable moments. I treasure them. Here are some of them:
